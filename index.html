    Snow depth

    This seasonLast seasonLast 5 seasons averageNormal (median)

    Inches of water the snowpack would make if it all melted, measured daily at the NRCS SNOTEL station on Wolf Creek Summit (10,930 ft), in the San Juan's headwaters above Pagosa. It usually peaks in late April and is gone by late June. Spring runoff through town tracks how much is up there. Early in the season the percentage swings a lot: one storm can double it. Snow depth is how deep it is on the ground; it normally peaks in March, about a month before the water does, because the pack settles and gets denser. Its normal is our own median of the station's 1998–2026 daily readings, smoothed over a week (NRCS doesn't publish one for depth). The gold line averages the last five complete seasons day by day (depth smoothed over a week too), so you can compare recent winters with the long-term normal.

    "New" is the rise in snow depth at the station, which undercounts a storm a little because fresh snow settles as it piles up; "settled" means the depth went down. Changes under 2″ show as 0″, since depth sensors wobble about an inch day to day. Station depth is natural snow in a forest clearing, not a resort's groomed base. Forecasts are for the resort's NWS grid square (about 2.5 km); the 7-day column adds up today and the next six days, with a small bar per day (hover or press for the amounts). Forecasts past about 3 days are much less certain. Always check the resort's own snow report: tap a resort's name to open its official site, where lift status (open or closed) and the snow report are on the home page.
